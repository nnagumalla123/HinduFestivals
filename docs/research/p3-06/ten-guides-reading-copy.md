# Diwali

*The festival of lights* · Also known as Deepavali

## Quick facts

- **Meaning:** “Row of lights” (from the Sanskrit dipavali), widely understood as light over darkness.
- **Length of observance:** Often described as five days. Lakshmi Puja, on the new-moon night, is a central observance for many communities. Counts and customs vary by region and family.
- **Associated deities:** Lakshmi (widely); Rama (northern India); Krishna (southern India and Govardhan Puja); Kali (Bengal, where the new-moon night is celebrated as Kali Puja). _(Varies by region)_
- **Common customs:** Lighting diyas, drawing rangoli, praying to Lakshmi, wearing new clothes, visiting family and friends, exchanging gifts, sharing sweets, and giving to those in need.

## Deities

- **Lakshmi** _(Widespread across Hindu traditions)_: Goddess of good fortune and wealth. Lamps are lit and doors left open to welcome her, and Lakshmi Puja is a central evening for many families.
- **Rama** _(Northern India)_: Hero of the Ramayana. His homecoming to Ayodhya is the best-known Diwali story in northern India.
- **Krishna** _(Southern India; Vaishnava traditions)_: Remembered for defeating the demon Narakasura (a southern Diwali story) and for lifting Govardhana Hill (Govardhan Puja).
- **Kali** _(Bengal)_: Worshipped in Bengal on the new-moon night of Diwali, a celebration known there as Kali Puja.

## Why celebrate Diwali?

Diwali, or Deepavali, means “row of lights,” and that is the whole spirit of it: a small flame held up against the dark. Homes get cleaned and decorated, diyas glow in windows and doorways, sweets get passed around, and many families welcome Lakshmi, the goddess of good fortune, into their homes.

Why people celebrate depends on the family. In much of northern India it is Rama’s homecoming to Ayodhya. In the south it is Krishna’s victory over the demon Narakasura. For many it is simply Lakshmi’s blessing on the year ahead. Jain and Sikh communities mark these days with their own meanings, too. The stories change from home to home, but the feeling does not: light beats darkness, and the year ahead feels full of possibility.

## Rama comes home to Ayodhya

_Tradition: Northern India (told widely elsewhere)_

In much of northern India, Diwali is the night Rama came home. After fourteen years of exile and the defeat of the demon king Ravana, Rama returned to Ayodhya with Sita, Lakshmana and Hanuman, and the tradition says the whole city lit lamps to welcome them. Families echo that welcome every year with rows of diyas.

Dussehra, earlier in the autumn, celebrates the victory over Ravana. Diwali is the homecoming.

## Krishna and the demon Narakasura

_Tradition: Southern India; Vaishnava tradition_

In many parts of southern India, the hero of Diwali is Krishna. The demon Narakasura had seized treasures from the gods and held many people captive, until Krishna, with his wife Satyabhama, defeated him and set the captives free. The victory is remembered on the day before the main Lakshmi Puja, and many families pray for their ancestors that day.

## Lakshmi finds her way in

_Tradition: Widespread; stories vary by community_

For many families, Diwali is Lakshmi’s night. She is the goddess of fortune and prosperity, and homes are lit up and left open to welcome her. Communities tell different stories about why: some say the festival celebrates her birth, others her marriage to Vishnu.

## Krishna lifts Govardhana Hill

_Tradition: Vaishnava traditions; the fourth day_

On the fourth day, many families remember Krishna lifting Govardhana Hill. When a furious storm sent by the god Indra threatened the cowherds of Vraja, Krishna held the hill up with one hand so that everyone could shelter beneath it for seven days. This fourth day is known as Govardhan Puja, after the hill.

## Diwali, day by day

Most people describe Diwali as five days, though the count and the customs on each day vary by region and family. Here is the common outline.

Day one, Dhanteras: a day for cleaning the house and buying something small and auspicious, like a little gold. Lakshmi is honored.

Day two, Naraka Chaturdashi (also called Choti Diwali): it remembers Krishna’s victory over Narakasura, and many families pray for their ancestors.

Day three, Lakshmi Puja: for many communities, the heart of the festival. On the new-moon night, families pray to Lakshmi, light lamps and candles, and visit temples.

Day four, Govardhan Puja: it recalls Krishna and the hill. In some regions, following the Vikrama calendar, it is also the first day of the new year, and merchants traditionally open fresh account books.

Day five, Bhai Dooj: a day to celebrate siblings, when sisters pray for their brothers’ well-being.

## The customs behind the glow

Diyas. These small clay or metal oil lamps line windowsills, doorways and temple steps, and some are floated on rivers. Lighting one is traditionally understood as a way to push back ignorance and welcome knowledge, purity and blessings.

Rangoli. Patterns made of colored rice, sand or flower petals decorate floors and doorways, a bright welcome for guests and good fortune.

Open doors. Doors and windows are left open so Lakshmi can find her way in.

New clothes and togetherness. Families dress up, visit one another and exchange gifts. Sharing sweets and festive meals with family and friends is a big part of the celebration. Giving is part of the spirit too, from feeding people in need to quiet acts of charity.

Fireworks. Fireworks are part of the festivities in many places, though practices vary by community and region, and in some places they are limited or avoided. If you take part, public displays and local safety rules are the best guide.

## Many Diwalis across India

_Tradition: Regional; varies by community_

There is no single Diwali, and the story you grew up with probably depends on your region and family. In Bengal, the same new-moon night is celebrated as Kali Puja, when the goddess Kali is worshipped. In some regions, such as Gujarat, Diwali opens the new year in the Vikrama calendar, while other communities begin their year at other times, so it is not the Hindu new year for everyone.

## Jain and Sikh meanings

_Tradition: Jain; Sikh_

Hindus are not the only ones who light lamps at this time of year. Jain and Sikh communities mark these days with their own meanings.

For Jains, Diwali remembers the night Mahavira, the last of the Tirthankaras, attained nirvana. Jain tradition recalls kings lighting lamps that night, since the light of his wisdom had left the world.

For Sikhs, these days are Bandi Chhor Divas, the “Day of Liberation.” It remembers Guru Hargobind’s release from imprisonment in Gwalior Fort. The tradition says he would not leave unless the 52 princes held with him were freed too, and when he returned to Amritsar the Golden Temple was lit in celebration. Today Sikhs gather at gurdwaras for prayer and langar, the community meal.

Each of these traditions tells its own story in its own terms.

## Celebrating Diwali away from home

You can celebrate meaningfully in a small room with a few smart choices.

Check your housing rules first. Many U.S. residence halls ban open flames, which can include candles, oil lamps and incense, and some limit decorations like string lights too. Read your housing contract or ask your residence life office before you light or hang anything. If you have questions about religious accommodations, ask your residence life office.

Get the glow without the flame. If your housing allows them, battery-powered LED diyas or tea lights give you the same warm light. Keep cords tidy, don’t chain extension cords together, and never cover a light with paper or cloth.

Make a rangoli on paper or poster board for your door or desk. It is a mess-free way to welcome guests.

Find your people. Look for a Hindu or Indian student association, a campus Diwali event or a nearby temple celebration. Or invite friends over, share sweets from an Indian grocery and tell them your family’s story. If you cook, check the rules and use a shared kitchen when you can.

Join the puja from afar. Video call your family at Lakshmi Puja time so you are part of it.

Give something back. Giving is part of Diwali, so consider volunteering or donating.

If fireworks are part of your family’s celebration, a public display is the simplest, safest way to enjoy them.

# Ugadi

*A new year for Telugu and Kannada families* · Also known as Yugadi

## Quick facts

- **Meaning:** “The beginning of an age” (yuga plus adi). The new year for many Telugu and Kannada communities. _(Telugu and Kannada)_
- **Length of observance:** One day, on the first day of the lunar month of Chaitra, at the start of spring.
- **Associated deities:** Brahma, in a traditional belief that the day marks the beginning of creation. _(Traditional belief)_
- **Common customs:** An oil bath and new clothes, mango-leaf doorway decorations, muggu (rangoli), temple visits, listening to the year’s almanac (Panchanga Sravanam), and tasting ugadi pachadi.
- **Signature foods:** Ugadi pachadi (Telugu) and bevu-bella (Kannada), mixes of sweet, bitter and other tastes whose ingredients vary by family and community. _(Telugu and Kannada)_

## Deities

- **Brahma** _(Traditional belief, as Britannica (some Hindu texts) and community sources describe it)_: The creator god. A traditional belief links Ugadi to the day he began creating the universe.

## Why celebrate Ugadi?

Ugadi is the new year for many Telugu and Kannada families, and it arrives with spring, on the first day of the lunar month of Chaitra. The name joins yuga (an age) and adi (a beginning), so it means “the beginning of an age.”

The day starts with an oil bath and new clothes. Fresh mango leaves hang over the door, a muggu (rangoli) greets guests, and families head to the temple, listen to the year’s almanac and taste ugadi pachadi, a blend of sweet, bitter, sour and more that stands for everything a year can hold. The idea is to take the sweet and the bitter together. In Maharashtra and Goa, the same day is called Gudi Padwa.

## A new year in spring

_Tradition: Telugu and Kannada communities_

Ugadi falls on the first day of Chaitra in the lunar calendar followed in Telugu- and Kannada-speaking regions, where each month ends on the new moon. That makes it the first day of the waxing moon, which is said to symbolize growth.

It is a seasonal turning point too, arriving with spring, a time associated with planting. In the Telugu calendar, years run in a cycle of sixty, and every year has its own name, so each Ugadi comes with a new name to learn.

Many families also see the day as a good moment for a fresh start.

## Brahma and the beginning of time

_Tradition: Traditional belief_

Many people link Ugadi to the very first morning of creation. According to a traditional belief, this is the day the god Brahma began creating the universe, which fits a festival whose name means “the beginning of an age.”

## How the day unfolds

_Tradition: Telugu and Kannada households_

Fresh start. Homes are cleaned, and the morning begins with a traditional oil bath and new clothes.

Mango leaves and muggu. Strings of mango leaves (toranalu) hang over doors, and muggu, also called rangoli or kolam, is drawn at the entrance.

Temple and prayer. Many families visit a temple and offer prayers.

The year’s almanac. Families often hear the panchangam, the traditional almanac, read aloud at a temple or by a respected elder. This is Panchanga Sravanam, and it includes the year’s forecasts, such as weather, harvests and general fortune.

## Ugadi pachadi: the taste of a year (Telugu)

_Tradition: Telugu_

Ugadi pachadi is the dish most closely tied to the day, and in many Telugu homes it is the first thing eaten. It blends several tastes, and the usual lineup is jaggery (sweet), raw mango (tangy), tamarind (sour), neem flowers (bitter), chili (spicy) and salt. Families make it their own way, though.

The idea is that a year holds all kinds of experiences, and you taste them together. In one popular reading, jaggery stands for joy, neem for hardship, raw mango for surprise, tamarind for disappointment, chili for anger or passion, and salt for balance. Other families read the tastes a little differently. There is also a playful belief that if you get more neem in your bite your year will be bitter, and if you get more jaggery it will be sweet.

## Bevu-bella in Karnataka (Kannada)

_Tradition: Kannada_

In Karnataka, the signature custom is bevu-bella, “neem and jaggery.” Neem flowers stand for the bitter and jaggery for the sweet, and families eat the mixture early in the day as a reminder that life holds both joy and hardship. Ugadi pachadi, the fuller multi-flavored mix, is especially popular in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, though many Karnataka homes make it too, and some families use the name bevu-bella for that fuller mix as well.

## Ugadi, Gudi Padwa and Chaitra Navaratri

_Tradition: Telugu, Kannada, Marathi and Konkani communities_

In Maharashtra and Goa, Marathi and Konkani communities celebrate the same day as Gudi Padwa. Their signature custom is the gudi: a cloth tied to a tall stick, topped with an upturned pot, neem and mango leaves and flowers, raised outside the home as a sign of victory and good fortune.

Across India, many communities also begin Chaitra Navaratri on the same lunar day, a spring version of Navaratri whose ninth day is Rama Navami. Because communities begin their year at different times, Ugadi is the new year for Telugu and Kannada families, not for every Hindu.

## Celebrating Ugadi away from home

Ugadi can slip by on a busy weekday, so give it a little plan.

Find your people. Look for a Telugu or Kannada student association, or ask whether a nearby temple has a program. If you do not have a community nearby, invite friends over and tell them what Ugadi means to your family.

Make a small pachadi. There is no single recipe, so a simple version in the spirit of the dish is fine. Ask a family member or a local Indian grocery what is available, since neem flowers can be hard to find. Cooking in dorm rooms is often restricted, so use a shared kitchen if your housing allows.

Decorate the doorway. Draw a muggu on paper or poster board for your door or desk. If fresh mango leaves are hard to find, a paper or cloth toran does the job.

Start something new. Pick one small goal for the year.

Call home. Ask a relative to read out the year’s panchangam, or just call and hear how everyone is celebrating.

If you want to light a lamp or incense, check your housing rules first. Many residence halls ban open flames, and a battery-powered lamp is a safer choice. If you have questions about religious accommodations, ask your residence life office.

# Holi

*The festival of colors*

## Quick facts

- **Meaning:** A Hindu spring festival of colors, preceded by an evening bonfire called Holika Dahan. It is celebrated as the triumph of good over evil and in memory of Radha and Krishna’s play.
- **Length of observance:** A day of color, preceded by the bonfire evening of Holika Dahan. Some regions celebrate for much longer. _(Varies by region)_
- **Associated deities:** Krishna and Radha; Vishnu through the story of Prahlada. _(Varies by region)_
- **Common customs:** A bonfire the evening before, throwing colored powder and water, music and dancing, sweets, and visiting friends and family.
- **Signature foods:** Gujiya and other sweets, and thandai, a cooling milk drink flavored with nuts and spices.

## Deities

- **Krishna** _(Braj Vaishnava tradition)_: Holi’s color play is said to recall his play with Radha and the gopis, especially in Mathura, Vrindavan and Barsana.
- **Radha** _(Braj Vaishnava tradition)_: Krishna’s beloved, whose playful love with him the day of color recalls.
- **Vishnu** _(Vaishnava tradition (the Prahlada story))_: Worshipped by the young Prahlada; in the story behind the bonfire, his devotee is saved from the fire and Vishnu, as the man-lion Narasimha, ends the tyrant king.

## Why celebrate Holi?

Holi is the Hindu spring festival best known for its colors. The evening before, communities light a bonfire called Holika Dahan, recalling the story of Prahlada and Holika. On Holi itself, people throw colored powder and water at one another, share sweets and music, and visit friends and family, in a celebration of spring, joy and the triumph of good over evil. Many also remember the playful love of Radha and Krishna in the throwing of color.

Several stories are tied to Holi, and each region leans on its own. Today it is also enjoyed by many people who are not Hindu, in India and around the world.

## The burning of Holika

_Tradition: Vaishnava tradition (the story of Prahlada)_

The best-known story behind the bonfire is about Prahlada. His father, the demon king Hiranyakashipu, had won a boon that made him almost impossible to kill, and he demanded that everyone worship him. Prahlada, however, was a devoted worshipper of Vishnu. Furious, the king asked his sister Holika, who was said to be immune to fire, to sit with Prahlada on a burning pyre; in some tellings she wore a protective cloak. Because of Prahlada’s devotion, the protection passed to him, and Holika burned.

Later, Vishnu appeared as the man-lion Narasimha and ended the king’s rule. The bonfire of Holika Dahan recalls this victory of good over evil. Like many such stories, it is told in several versions.

## Radha and Krishna

_Tradition: Braj Vaishnava tradition_

Holi is especially dear to worshippers of Krishna, whose play with Radha and the gopis, the young women of Vrindavan, it is said to recall. One popular story says that the young Krishna playfully smeared color on Radha’s face, and the play grew into the festival.

In Mathura, Vrindavan and Barsana, the places linked with Krishna’s life, Holi is celebrated with particular joy.

## A bonfire and a day of color

Holi is preceded by a related observance, Holika Dahan, on the evening before. Families and neighbors gather around a bonfire, sing, and offer roasted grains as a way of letting go of the past year’s negativity.

The next day is the day of color. People throw gulal, colored powder, and squirt colored water from buckets and water guns called pichkaris, with music, dancing and shouts of “Holi hai!” Many wear white. In some places the play loosens everyday formalities of age and status, though customs vary and not every community plays in the same way. When it ends, people bathe, put on clean clothes and visit friends, teachers and relatives, often with sweets.

## Colors, then and now

Holi colors were traditionally made from plants: green from neem and henna leaves, yellow from turmeric and marigold, blue from indigo, and red from pomegranate, flame-of-the-forest flowers and red sandalwood. Today synthetic powders are common too. Ingredients vary from product to product, and Britannica cautions that some contain pigments that can irritate the skin and eyes, so some people choose herbal colors.

The colors are also said to carry meanings: green for new life, red for marriage and fertility, yellow for purity and victory, and blue for Krishna.

## Sweets and drinks

_Tradition: Varies by region_

Sharing food is part of the day. Popular sweets include gujiya, a fried pastry filled with coconut and dried fruit; jalebi, a syrup-soaked spiral; and malpua, a pancake dipped in syrup. In Maharashtra, puran poli, a sweet flatbread, is served. Savory snacks such as chaat and kachori are common too.

Drinks include lassi and thandai, a cooling milk drink flavored with almonds, saffron, poppy seeds, cardamom and rose petals. Bhang, a mild cannabis-based drink, is listed separately from thandai and is served in some places.

## Holi across India

_Tradition: Regional; varies by community_

Holi takes different forms across India, lasting anywhere from two days to many weeks. In Braj, the region around Mathura and Vrindavan, the celebrations begin with Basant Panchami and build for weeks. At Barsana, Lathmar Holi has women of Radha’s village playfully strike men from Krishna’s village with staves while the men shield themselves.

In West Bengal and Odisha the festival is Dol Jatra, the “swing festival,” when images of Radha and Krishna are placed on decorated platforms and swung to songs of spring. In Punjab, Sikhs celebrate Hola Mohalla the day after Holi, a separate festival marked by displays of martial skill.

## Holi beyond temples and homes

_Tradition: Diaspora and pan-Indian_

Holi has become popular in South Asian communities in the United States and elsewhere, and many people who are not Hindu join in; the Government of India’s tourism site describes it as a festival that crosses religion and age. The Hindu American Foundation says Holi is open to everyone and that understanding its origin can enrich the experience, noting that commercial color events can lose that context.

In some places the loosening of everyday boundaries during the festival has led to harassment and fights, and police in many places in India take precautions. Ask before putting color on someone, and respect a no.

## Celebrating Holi away from home

You can enjoy Holi with a few simple choices.

Find a celebration. Look for a Hindu or Indian student association event or a community gathering with outdoor space.

Dress for it. Wear old clothes you do not mind staining, and keep colors outdoors, since powders can stain floors and walls.

Protect your eyes and skin. Color ingredients vary, so check labels or choose herbal colors if you prefer, and keep powder away from your eyes. If color gets in your eyes, do not rub them and flush the eye right away with plenty of clean water. If a liquid color, dye or other chemical splashes into your eye, the American Academy of Ophthalmology advises seeking emergency medical treatment right away. For ordinary dust or grit, blinking and flushing often clears it, but see a doctor if it still feels like something is in your eye. Ongoing pain or trouble seeing is a sign to get medical help right away.

Ask first. Always ask before putting color on someone, and respect a no.

Leave the bonfire to organized events. Many residence halls ban open flames, so check your housing contract. If you have questions about religious accommodations, ask your residence life office.

Call home. A video call with family keeps you part of the celebration.

# Ganesh Chaturthi

*A festival honoring the birth of Ganesha* · Also known as Vinayaka Chaturthi

## Quick facts

- **Meaning:** A festival celebrating the birth of Ganesha, the elephant-headed god of beginnings and remover of obstacles. It begins on the fourth day (chaturthi) of the month of Bhadrapada.
- **Length of observance:** Counting the first day as day one, the festival can run to the eleventh day, called Anant Chaturdashi. Sources also call it a ten-day festival because it ends ten days after the first. Many homes keep Ganesha for a shorter, odd number of days: one and a half, three, five or seven, as the family’s tradition suggests. _(Varies by family and community)_
- **Associated deities:** Ganesha, with his parents Shiva and Parvati in his birth stories. _(Puranic traditions)_
- **Common customs:** Welcoming a clay image of Ganesha into the home or a community pandal, daily worship and offerings, and a final farewell, with the image carried to water for immersion.
- **Signature foods:** Modaks, sweet dumplings regarded as Ganesha’s favorite, along with coconut, jaggery and other round sweets such as laddus, offered first and then shared as prasada. _(Regional; varies by community)_

## Deities

- **Ganesha** _(Hindu traditions; Puranic birth stories vary)_: The elephant-headed god of beginnings, traditionally worshipped before any major undertaking and honored as a remover of obstacles. His festival celebrates his birth.
- **Parvati** _(Puranic tradition; versions vary)_: In the best-known birth story, Ganesha’s mother, who makes him from the dirt of her body to guard her while she bathes. In that version she alone makes him, though he is traditionally regarded as the child of both Parvati and Shiva.
- **Shiva** _(Puranic tradition; versions vary)_: Parvati’s husband and, in tradition, Ganesha’s father. In the best-known story he replaces the boy’s lost head with an elephant’s head.

## Why celebrate Ganesh Chaturthi?

Ganesh Chaturthi celebrates the birth of Ganesha, the elephant-headed god worshipped as the remover of obstacles and the god of beginnings. Many Hindus invoke him before starting a new task, a journey or a ritual, so welcoming him at the start of a festival feels fitting.

The festival begins on the fourth day of the month of Bhadrapada. A clay image of Ganesha is installed in a home or in a decorated community pandal, worshipped for a number of days, and then carried in procession to water for immersion, a farewell that symbolizes his journey home. It is a quiet family observance in some houses and a large public celebration in others, especially in Maharashtra, and it is observed by Hindu communities around the world.

## Ganesha’s birth: more than one story

_Tradition: Shiva Purana and other Puranic traditions; versions vary_

Many different stories are told about how Ganesha was born. One well-known telling is in the Shiva Purana, in its Rudra Samhita. There Parvati’s companions Jaya and Vijaya remind her that Shiva’s attendants serve only him, and she decides to have a servant of her own. From the dirt of her body she makes a handsome young man, gives him clothes and a staff, and sets him as gatekeeper while she bathes. When Shiva comes to the door, the young man does not recognize him and bars his way. The account’s opening says that in one version Ganesha’s head was cut off by Shiva and an elephant’s head was put on him. In this telling Ganesha is born of Parvati alone, though he is traditionally regarded as the child of both Parvati and Shiva.

The Purana itself says the story differs from one age to another, and other tellings differ. In one Parvati makes her son from a piece of cloth and asks Shiva to bring him to life, and in some variants the planet Saturn’s glance, not Shiva, is the cause of the lost head. HAF notes that stories vary on how Ganesha got his elephant head. This guide does not choose one version as the true one.

## Ganesha, the remover of obstacles

_Tradition: Hindu traditions_

Ganesha’s name means “Lord of the People” and “Lord of the Ganas,” the hosts that attend Shiva. He is potbellied and usually shown holding sweets, with a rat as his vehicle. Britannica notes that, like a rat and like an elephant, he is a remover of obstacles, and HAF reads the rat as a picture of the wandering mind that devotees try to steady. He is worshipped before a new beginning, and Brandeis describes him as known for making the impossible possible and filling life with success and happiness.

Because he is invoked first in many rituals, the festival of his birth is one of the most popular in the Hindu calendar. Families and communities see it as a chance to begin the season with his blessing.

## Welcoming Ganesha

Before the festival, artisans make and sell Ganesha images, from small figures that families may make at home to very large ones. At the start, an image is placed on a raised platform in a home shrine or in a decorated community pandal. The worship begins with prana pratishtha, a ritual to invite life into the image, followed by the sixteen-step worship called shodashopachara. Mantras and hymns are chanted, including the Ganapati Atharvashirsha, and the image is anointed with red sandalwood paste and decorated with flowers.

The image is worshipped for an odd number of days. In homes the celebration can end after one and a half, three, five or seven days as a family’s tradition suggests. In some homes and in larger public celebrations it lasts until Anant Chaturdashi, which is the eleventh day if the first day is counted as day one and ten days after the first, and this is why sources call it a ten-day festival. At the end a final puja, the uttarapuja, says goodbye to Ganesha. The image is then carried to water in a family walk or a large procession with drums, singing and dancing and immersed in an act called visarjan, understood as Ganesha’s journey home to Mount Kailash, where his parents Shiva and Parvati live.

## Modaks and sweets

_Tradition: Regional; varies by community_

Offerings to Ganesha include coconut, jaggery, and 21 modaks, sweet dumplings that Britannica says are considered his favorite food. Hinduism Today describes the modaka in his hand as a symbol of enlightened attainments. Laddus and other round sweets are offered too, and once blessed they are shared with everyone as prasada. HAF adds that prasada is distributed throughout the community during the festival. What a family prepares varies with its region and tradition.

## How the festival became a public celebration

_Tradition: Maharashtra; historical_

Brut describes Ganesh Chaturthi as largely a private celebration before the late nineteenth century, though Ganesh celebrations in Pune received royal patronage under the Peshwas until their fall in 1818. The festival took its modern public form in the 1890s. Britannica credits the independence leader Bal Gangadhar Tilak with reviving it as a large public event in Maharashtra in 1893, when the British banned political assemblies. Tilak praised the public celebration in his Marathi newspaper Kesari, and mandals, community groups, collected funds locally and installed large images in decorated pavilions.

Who first took the festival public is still debated. Some research credits Bhausaheb Rangari with a public celebration in Pune as early as 1892, and writers disagree on whether 1892 or 1893 marks the beginning, so this guide does not treat either year as settled. What the sources agree on is that the public festival grew in the 1890s and spread to many towns.

Today the public festival is especially large in Maharashtra, including Mumbai and Pune, and is celebrated in many other parts of India and by Hindu communities worldwide.

## Home shrines, pandals and communities

_Tradition: Regional; varies by community_

In Maharashtra the festival is especially prominent. Cities such as Mumbai and Pune host very large community celebrations, and Hinduism Today calls Mumbai the place where the festival is observed with the most creativity and enthusiasm. Hindu Press International lists Maharashtra, Gujarat, Odisha, Uttar Pradesh and Karnataka among the many states where it is celebrated. Britannica notes that it is celebrated with pomp and devotion in Hindu communities worldwide.

Within a single town the festival can look very different from house to house. A small home image may be kept for a day and a half, while a neighborhood mandal’s large image stays for ten days or more. Neither is more correct than the other; a family or community follows its own tradition.

## Environmentally responsible immersion

_Tradition: Guidance from India and for communities elsewhere_

Immersing images in rivers, lakes and the sea has raised environmental concerns. Britannica notes that many large public statues are made of plaster of paris, which does not break down, and painted with chemical paints that contain metals such as lead, mercury and chromium. In India, environmental groups and local governments encourage images made of dissolving clay or soil and decorated with natural colors, and pollution-control authorities have issued guidelines that discourage plaster of paris, plastic and toxic paints and favor designated immersion tanks. Reports say these guidelines have been unevenly followed and that large community groups find clay images heavy and fragile.

Households can choose a small, unbaked clay image painted with natural colors or left unpainted, and can remove decorations that do not dissolve before the farewell. Hinduism Today notes that the image is returned to the water after non-degradable decorations are removed. Rules and arrangements for immersion differ from place to place, so a temple or community organizer is the best guide to what is appropriate locally.

## Celebrating Ganesh Chaturthi away from home

You can welcome Ganesha even in a small room.

Find your people. Ask a Hindu or Indian student association or a nearby temple whether it hosts an installation, aarti or immersion, and whether you can join online.

Choose a small clay image. A small, unbaked clay Ganesha with natural colors, or an unpainted one, is easy to keep in a room and easy to immerse responsibly. You can keep him for as many days as suit your family’s tradition and your schedule.

Worship without a flame. Many residence halls ban open flames, including candles, oil lamps and incense, so check your housing contract and use a battery-powered lamp and fresh flowers. If you have questions about religious accommodations, ask your residence life office.

Plan the farewell with a temple or community organizer, who will know what local arrangements are appropriate. Rules on immersing images in lakes, rivers or the sea differ from place to place.

Share sweets thoughtfully. Modaks and other sweets can contain coconut or nuts, so tell friends what is in what you share.

Call home. A video call with family during the puja keeps you part of the celebration.

# Navaratri

*Nine nights honoring the goddess* · Also known as Navratri

## Quick facts

- **Meaning:** “Nine nights” (nava plus ratri), a festival honoring the divine feminine, centered on the goddess Durga.
- **Length of observance:** Nine nights, followed by the tenth day, Vijayadashami.
- **Associated deities:** Durga, honored in her nine forms in many traditions; in many others the nights go to Durga, Lakshmi and Saraswati in turn. _(Varies by tradition)_
- **Common customs:** Worship of the goddess, garba and dandiya dancing, Durga Puja pavilions, Golu doll displays, honoring young girls (Kanya Puja), and for some, fasting.
- **Dietary practices:** Some devotees choose simple vegetarian (sattvic) meals, and some fast. Fasting is optional and varies by region, family and person. _(Varies)_

## Deities

- **Durga** _(Shakta and wider Hindu traditions)_: The warrior goddess at the center of the festival, remembered for defeating the buffalo demon Mahishasura.
- **Lakshmi** _(Three-phase scheme (Durga, Lakshmi, Saraswati))_: Goddess of prosperity, honored in many traditions during the middle three nights.
- **Saraswati** _(Three-phase scheme; South India (ninth day))_: Goddess of knowledge and wisdom, honored in many traditions during the last three nights; books and instruments are honored on the ninth day in parts of South India.

## Why celebrate Navaratri?

Navaratri, “nine nights,” is a festival of the divine feminine, centered on the goddess Durga and her many forms. It is a season of devotion, music and dance, and, for some families, fasting. It ends with Vijayadashami on the tenth day. This guide covers Sharad Navaratri, the autumn one; a spring Chaitra Navaratri also exists.

At its heart is the story of the goddess defeating the buffalo demon Mahishasura, remembered as the victory of good over evil. How people celebrate depends on where their family is from: Gujarat dances garba, Bengal builds grand Durga Puja pavilions, many South Indian families arrange dolls on stepped displays, and many homes honor young girls as the goddess herself. In many traditions the nights are shared among Durga, Lakshmi and Saraswati, for power, prosperity and wisdom.

## Durga and Mahishasura

_Tradition: Shakta tradition; the Devi Mahatmya_

The best-known Navaratri story comes from the Devi Mahatmya, an ancient text about the goddess. The buffalo demon Mahishasura, protected by a boon that no male could defeat him, had driven the gods from heaven. Unable to beat him, the gods pooled their powers to create a warrior goddess, Durga, and armed her with their own weapons. She rode a lion into battle and defeated him.

Many people connect that victory with the festival, and with Vijayadashami, the “victorious tenth.”

## Nine nights, three phases

Many families divide the nights into three phases of three: the first for Durga, the second for Lakshmi, and the third for Saraswati, often seen as strength, prosperity and wisdom. Others worship nine forms of Durga, one for each night, with names like Shailaputri, Brahmacharini and Kalaratri. Which pattern a family follows varies.

A much-loved custom is Kanya Puja, or Kanjak, on the eighth or ninth day. Young girls are honored as forms of the goddess: invited into the home, their feet washed, an arti performed, and gifts given.

There is no single right way to do Navaratri: some people follow three goddesses, others nine forms of Durga, and some visit a temple, dance garba or set up a Golu.

## Fasting and food

_Tradition: Varies by region, family and person_

Fasting is part of Navaratri for many people, but it is optional and takes many forms. Some devotees choose simple vegetarian (sattvic) meals, and some keep a daytime fast. In parts of North India many people fast. Some families do not fast, and what any family does is shaped by its own tradition.

Food is part of the worship as well. At home, many devotees offer bhog, food prepared for the goddess, to her image. At Kanya Puja, the girls are traditionally served puri, semolina halva and black chickpeas.

## Durga Puja in Bengal and eastern India

_Tradition: Bengal and eastern India_

In West Bengal, Assam and other eastern states, Navaratri takes the form of Durga Puja. The festival is traditionally ten days long and opens with Mahalaya, which heralds the goddess’s arrival. Worship begins on the sixth day, Shashthi, and the last five days, through Vijayadashami, are the main period of veneration. Huge clay images of Durga fighting Mahishasura are installed in elaborately decorated pandals, or temporary pavilions, and in temples, and concerts, plays and fairs fill the days.

Vijayadashami brings the farewell. A mirror ritual marks the goddess’s presence leaving the image, married women apply vermilion to the goddess and to one another, and the images are carried in processions and immersed in water.

A popular belief holds that Durga comes home for these days to visit her family, and the immersion at the end is understood as her return to her husband, Shiva, in the Himalayas.

## Garba and dandiya in Gujarat

_Tradition: Gujarat and the Gujarati diaspora_

In Gujarat, Navaratri is closely tied to garba and dandiya raas, circle dances done in vibrant clothes, often around a lamp. Fairs and feasts bring neighborhoods together. In the United States, communities hold large public garba celebrations, and for many young people, garba nights are a lively way to take part.

## Golu in South India

_Tradition: Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Karnataka_

In parts of South India, families arrange dolls and figures on stepped platforms, usually in an odd number of tiers. The displays show deities and scenes from the Ramayana, and families invite friends and neighbors over to see them, exchange offerings and sing devotional songs. The display is also called Bommai Golu.

The ninth day brings Ayudha Puja, when the goddesses Lakshmi, Saraswati and Parvati are worshipped and tools, instruments and books are cleaned, decorated and honored as the means of work and learning.

## Celebrating Navaratri away from home

Navaratri travels well, because so much of it is community and so much is up to you. A good way to start is to pick the thread that feels closest to your family, whether that is garba, a mini Golu or a small puja, and follow it for a night or two.

Find the dancing. Many cities and campuses hold garba and dandiya nights. Ask your Indian or Hindu student association, or look for a local temple or community event.

Build a mini Golu. A small display on a shelf, with a few figures or pictures, works. Invite friends over to see it, as families traditionally invite neighbors, and use a battery-powered lamp for light.

Check your housing rules. Many residence halls ban open flames, including candles, oil lamps and incense, and some limit decorations like string lights. Read your housing contract before lighting or hanging anything. If you have questions about religious accommodations, ask your residence life office.

Honor your tools. This is a modern adaptation, not a traditional rule. In South India, Ayudha Puja honors tools, instruments and books as means of work and learning, and many students clean and decorate their pens, instruments or books. If you like, on the ninth day you could tidy your desk, wipe down your laptop and place a flower by your books or instrument in that spirit.

If you choose to fast. Some people keep a full fast, some choose simple vegetarian meals, and some celebrate without fasting, so do whatever fits your family’s tradition and your own life. If you do change what you eat, your dining hall or a nearby Indian grocery can help with ingredients, and in-room cooking is often restricted.

Stay connected. A video call with family, or sharing a meal over a call, keeps you part of the celebration back home.

# Vijayadashami

*The victorious tenth day* · Also known as Dussehra, Dasara, Vijaya Dashami

## Quick facts

- **Meaning:** “Vijayadashami” means “the tenth day of victory.” In much of India the day is known as Dussehra, from the Sanskrit dasha, “ten,” and hara, “defeat,” remembering Rama’s victory over the ten-headed Ravana. In Bengal and the east it is the last day of Durga Puja, honoring Durga’s victory over Mahishasura.
- **Length of observance:** One day, the tenth after the nine nights of Navaratri. Some celebrations, such as Ramlila performances, Durga Puja and Mysuru Dasara, build up over many days before it. _(Varies by region)_
- **Associated deities:** Rama, in the Ramayana’s story of Ravana’s defeat, and Durga, in the story of Mahishasura’s defeat. In Mysuru, the goddess is honored as Chamundeshwari. _(Varies by tradition and region)_
- **Common customs:** Ramlila performances and the burning of Ravana effigies in the north; the farewell and immersion of Durga images in Bengal and the east; the Jamboo Savari procession in Mysuru; and the exchange of apta leaves in Maharashtra. Ayudha Puja, the worship of tools and books, falls the day before, on Mahanavami. _(Regional)_

## Deities

- **Rama** _(Vaishnava tradition; the Ramayana)_: The hero of the Ramayana, who defeats the ten-headed demon king Ravana to rescue his wife Sita. His victory is the story behind Dussehra in much of India and is retold in Ramlila.
- **Durga** _(Shakta tradition; Durga Puja in the east)_: The warrior goddess who defeats the buffalo demon Mahishasura, as told in the Devi Mahatmya. Vijayadashami is the last day of Durga Puja, when images of the goddess are bid farewell and immersed.
- **Chamundeshwari** _(Mysuru and Karnataka)_: The goddess honored in Mysuru Dasara, remembered for defeating Mahishasura. She is carried in procession on Vijayadashami.

## Why celebrate Vijayadashami?

Vijayadashami, “the victorious tenth,” falls on the day after the nine nights of Navaratri. It is a festival of victory, and different parts of India remember different victories. In much of the north and west it is Dussehra, when Rama’s victory over the ten-headed king Ravana is celebrated, and the story is told in Ramlila plays and by burning effigies. In Bengal and the east it is the last day of Durga Puja, when the goddess Durga, who defeated the buffalo demon Mahishasura, is bid farewell.

Many people see in these stories a reminder that good prevails over evil and that each person can overcome their own faults. In the south and in Maharashtra, the days around it are also a time to honor tools, books and, in some places, weapons. The names, stories and customs vary by region, and this guide keeps them apart.

## Vijayadashami, Dussehra and Dasara: the names

_Tradition: Varies by region and tradition_

Vijayadashami, Dussehra and Dasara are different names, used in different regions, for the same tenth day. Britannica explains that Dussehra, from the Sanskrit dasha, “ten,” and hara, “defeat,” is the name used in much of the north and west, where the day marks the end of Sharad Navratri and celebrates Rama’s victory over Ravana. In West Bengal, Assam and other eastern states, Vijayadashami, also written Vijaya Dashami and known in Bengali as Bijoya Doshomi, is the tenth and final day of Durga Puja. In Karnataka the festival is known as Dasara, and Mysuru’s celebration is its best-known form. Britannica notes that Dussehra and Vijayadashami are often used interchangeably, perhaps because both mark a triumph of good over evil, though each name carries its own emphasis.

The day follows the nine nights of Navaratri, and for many people it also begins the run-up to Diwali, which in much of the north remembers Rama’s return to Ayodhya. Dussehra celebrates the victory; Diwali, the homecoming.

## Rama and Ravana

_Tradition: Vaishnava tradition; the Ramayana_

The Ramayana tells how Ravana, the ten-headed king of Lanka, abducted Rama’s wife Sita. Rama, with his brother Lakshmana and allies including the monkey king Sugriva, the commander Hanuman and Ravana’s own brother Vibhishana, waged war to rescue her. In Valmiki’s Sanskrit epic, as translated by K. M. K. Murthy, Rama’s charioteer Matali urges him to use a weapon of Brahma, and Rama’s arrow pierces Ravana’s heart and brings him down from his chariot. HAF and Brandeis describe Dussehra as celebrating this victory.

Ramlila, the folk theater of the Ramayana, is based on Tulsidas’s sixteenth-century retelling, the Ramacharitmanas, rather than on Valmiki. Many families and communities tell the story in their own ways, and some traditions tell it with different emphases.

## Durga and Mahishasura

_Tradition: Shakta tradition; the Devi Mahatmya_

In the east and in many Shakta traditions, Vijayadashami recalls the goddess Durga’s victory over the buffalo demon Mahishasura. Britannica says the story first appears in the Devi Mahatmya, part of the Markandeya Purana, in which the gods combine their powers to create a warrior goddess who defeats him. Durga Puja, which celebrates this victory, ends on Vijayadashami, when the goddess is bid farewell. The Navaratri guide tells the story in more detail.

Some communities remember both victories on the same day. In one ISKCON community described by its communications director, the tenth day recalls Durga’s victory over Mahishasura as well as Rama’s over Ravana.

## Ramlila and the burning of Ravana

_Tradition: Northern and western India_

In northern and western India, Ramlila, a dramatic folk retelling of the Ramayana with narration, song and dialogue, leads up to the day. UNESCO describes performances lasting ten to twelve days, and some, such as the one at Ramnagar, up to a month. The best-known are at Ayodhya, Ramnagar and Benares, where villagers play roles and the audience is invited to sing along.

Most Ramlilas end with Ravana’s defeat, and then comes Ravana dahan, “the burning of Ravana.” Large effigies of Ravana, often alongside his son Meghanada and his brother Kumbhakarna, are set alight at dusk in open fields. Britannica notes that the effigies are often stuffed with firecrackers. Brandeis says the burning reminds people to overcome their own vices.

## Mahanavami and Vijayadashami: tools, books, shami and apta leaves

_Tradition: South India, Karnataka and Maharashtra_

On Mahanavami, the ninth day of Navratri, Ayudha Puja is observed in many southern states, when tools, instruments and objects of knowledge are honored. Across the nine nights, southern homes also set up Golu, a display of dolls and figures on stepped platforms. In Mysuru, the Mahanavami program includes Ayudha puja, and the royal family worships weapons and royal vehicles.

Vijayadashami itself brings different customs. In Mysuru it includes a shami puja and the Jamboo Savari procession. In Maharashtra, people exchange the leaves of the apta tree on Dussehra and call them sona, “gold,” as a gesture of goodwill, with the words “accept this gold and remain like gold.” Outlook Traveller links the custom to a tradition that the Pandavas hid their weapons in a shami tree, and to the Maratha practice of soldiers returning from campaigns with apta or shami leaves for the women of their families. The shami tree is also worshipped in several other parts of India.

## Bengal, Mysuru, Kullu and more

_Tradition: Regional; varies by community_

In West Bengal, Assam and other eastern states Vijayadashami is the last day of Durga Puja. At dawn a mirror ritual, darpan visarjan, marks the goddess’s presence leaving the image. Married women then apply sindoor, vermilion, to the goddess and to one another in sindoor khela, and the images are carried in processions to water and immersed, with sweets offered for the goddess’s journey back to Mount Kailash.

In Mysuru, Dasara is the state festival of Karnataka. It honors the goddess Chamundeshwari’s victory over Mahishasura and ends on Vijayadashami with the Jamboo Savari, a procession from the Palace in which the goddess travels on a golden howdah carried by an elephant. Karnataka Tourism traces the festival to the Vijayanagara rulers and says a Wadiyar king revived it in the early seventeenth century.

In the Kullu valley of Himachal Pradesh, Britannica says that instead of burning effigies of Ravana, local deities are carried in procession at a week-long fair, with Raghunath, a form of Rama, as the presiding deity.

## Celebrating Vijayadashami away from home

You can mark Vijayadashami with a few simple choices.

Find your people. Ask a Hindu or Indian student association or a nearby temple whether it hosts a Ramlila, a Ravana dahan, a Durga farewell or a Dasara gathering, and whether it streams one.

Watch effigy burnings from a safe distance. Effigies may be stuffed with firecrackers, so stay behind barriers and leave the lighting to organizers who have permits and safety crews. Do not light fireworks yourself; many places and residence halls restrict them.

Optionally, adapt Ayudha Puja. Some students place their books or laptop before a small picture and offer a flower on Mahanavami or Vijayadashami. This is a modern adaptation, not a documented traditional ritual, so follow your family’s guidance. Many residence halls ban open flames, including candles, oil lamps and incense, so check your housing contract. If you have questions about religious accommodations, ask your residence life office.

Share goodwill. A friendly exchange of sweets or leaves, in the spirit of the Maharashtra custom, is a gentle way to join in.

Call home. A video call with family keeps you part of the celebration.

# Krishna Janmashtami

*A festival honoring the birth of Krishna* · Also known as Janmashtami, Gokulashtami

## Quick facts

- **Meaning:** “Janmashtami” joins janma, “birth,” and ashtami, “the eighth day.” The festival celebrates the birth of Krishna, regarded in Vaishnava tradition as an avatar of Vishnu, on the eighth day of the waning fortnight of the month of Bhadrapada.
- **Length of observance:** One day and night, centered on a midnight celebration. In Maharashtra and Goa, Dahi Handi follows the next day, and in Vrindavan some festivities begin about ten days earlier. Observance days can differ by tradition. _(Varies by region and tradition)_
- **Associated deities:** Krishna, an avatar of Vishnu in Vaishnava tradition. His parents, Devaki and Vasudeva, and his foster parents, Yashoda and Nanda, appear in his birth story. _(Vaishnava traditions)_
- **Common customs:** A midnight celebration of the birth, bathing and dressing an image of baby Krishna, devotional singing, scenes of his childhood, and sweets shared as prasada. In some places, pots of curd are broken by human pyramids the next day.
- **Dietary practices:** Many devotees fast until midnight and then share prasada. _(Varies by family and tradition)_

## Deities

- **Krishna** _(Vaishnava traditions; Bhagavata Purana)_: Born in Mathura to Devaki and Vasudeva and raised in Gokula by Yashoda and Nanda, as told in the Bhagavata Purana. Vaishnava traditions regard him as an avatar of Vishnu, and some, such as Gaudiya Vaishnavism, hold his birth festival especially important.
- **Vishnu** _(Vaishnava tradition; Bhagavata Purana)_: In the Bhagavata Purana’s account, Vishnu takes birth as Krishna to relieve the earth of cruel rulers.

## Why celebrate Janmashtami?

Janmashtami celebrates the birth of Krishna, the beloved god of the Bhagavata Purana, who is regarded in Vaishnava tradition as an avatar of Vishnu. The festival falls on the eighth day of the waning moon in the month of Bhadrapada, and its heart is a midnight celebration, the hour in which tradition places his birth.

Devotees keep a vigil, often fast until midnight, then bathe and dress an image of baby Krishna, sing, and share sweets as prasada. Temples and homes are decorated, and in many places tableaux show scenes from his childhood. The day after, in Maharashtra and Goa and some other places, young people form human pyramids to break a pot of curd. How a family keeps the day, and even which day it keeps, depends on its tradition and region.

## The birth of Krishna

_Tradition: Vaishnava tradition, as told in the Bhagavata Purana_

In the Bhagavata Purana’s account, the earth is burdened by cruel kings and Vishnu promises to be born to relieve it. In Mathura, the tyrant Kamsa learns from a voice in the sky that the eighth son of his sister Devaki will kill him. He imprisons Devaki and her husband Vasudeva and kills their first six children. Their seventh child, Balarama, is miraculously transferred to the womb of Rohini, another wife of Vasudeva.

When the eighth child is born, in the dark of night, the parents first see him in his divine form as Vishnu before he becomes an ordinary baby. He tells them to carry him across the Yamuna River to Gokula and to bring back the daughter just born to Yashoda and Nanda. As clouds thunder and rain falls, the prison doors open, the guards fall asleep, and the river makes way for Vasudeva. He leaves the baby with Yashoda and returns with the girl, who is Yogamaya, Vishnu’s divine power. When Kamsa tries to kill her she appears as a goddess and he is humbled. Krishna grows up in Gokula and Vrindavan and later defeats Kamsa.

This is one telling. Communities and traditions emphasize different parts of Krishna’s life and tell it with their own songs, plays and images.

## A night vigil and a midnight birth

On Janmashtami, many devotees keep a vigil through the day and evening, singing devotional songs and listening to stories of Krishna. At midnight, the traditional hour of his birth, temples and homes celebrate the moment with kirtan, conch shells and a short worship. An image of baby Krishna is bathed, often in water and milk, dressed in new clothes and placed in a cradle. In a ceremony called abhisheka, which temples often hold in public, the deity is ritually bathed.

Temples and homes are decorated with flowers, and many families set up jhankis, three-dimensional scenes of Krishna’s childhood showing the Yamuna, animals, and the people of his story. Some women draw small footprints at the doorway to welcome the child. Dances and dramas about Krishna’s life are performed, and after the midnight celebration, sweets and other food are first offered to the deity and then shared as prasada. In some places, public readings from the Bhagavata Purana or the Bhagavad Gita are part of the day.

## Fasting and the midnight vigil

_Tradition: Varies by family and tradition_

Many devotees fast on the day before the midnight celebration and break the fast with prasada after the birth is celebrated. HAF describes fasting as a way of focusing the senses; Brandeis describes people fasting, meditating, chanting and singing to Krishna through the day. At ISKCON’s temple in Vrindavan, devotees who fast still cook throughout the day to prepare the offering. Fasting practices vary by family and tradition.

## Curd, butter and sweets

_Tradition: Regional; varies by community_

Sweets and other foods are offered to Krishna first and then shared as prasada. Milk, curd and butter have a special place because Krishna’s childhood stories are full of cowherd boys and stolen butter, and the Dahi Handi pots hold curd. India TV describes milk and butter as typical prasada, ISKCON News describes a temple offering of 108 dishes, and Incredible India describes Chhappan Bhog, an offering of 56 dishes, in Puri. What a family or temple prepares depends on its tradition and region.

## Mathura, Vrindavan and beyond

_Tradition: Regional; varies by community_

The festival is observed especially in Mathura and Vrindavan, the places associated with Krishna’s birth and childhood. HAF notes that in Vrindavan some festivities begin about ten days before Krishna’s birthday, and many pilgrims travel there. Incredible India describes Rasa Lila, dance-dramas of Krishna’s life, as especially popular in the Mathura region, in Manipur and Assam, and in parts of Rajasthan and Gujarat.

In Puri, in Odisha, the Jagannath Temple marks the festival, and Incredible India describes an offering of 56 dishes there. In Maharashtra, Goa and Gujarat the day after Janmashtami brings Dahi Handi. Beyond India, Hindu communities, including ISKCON temples, hold midnight programs, dramas and tableaux of their own.

## Smarta and Vaishnava traditions: why the day can differ

_Tradition: Smarta and Vaishnava traditions_

Janmashtami is tied to the eighth day of the waning moon, and different traditions follow their own calendar practices in deciding which day that is. As a result, observance dates can differ by tradition: some communities mark Janmashtami on one day and others on the next. A report from Odisha describes Smarta temples and households and Vaishnava institutions observing on consecutive days in one recent year.

Which day a family follows is a matter of its own tradition. Temples usually announce theirs, and a local temple’s announcement is the best guide.

## Dahi Handi: the pot of curd

_Tradition: Maharashtra, Goa and western India_

In Maharashtra and Goa, the day after Janmashtami is marked by Dahi Handi, “the pot of curd.” A pot of curd is hung high above a street from a pole or wire, and groups of young people, called govindas after a name of Krishna, form a human pyramid to reach and break it. The game imitates the boy Krishna and his friends stealing curd from pots that their mothers hung out of reach.

Because the pyramids are tall and the climbers at the top are usually the lightest and youngest, Dahi Handi has caused injuries. It is best watched from a safe distance, and anyone who takes part should do so only in a well-organized event that follows the local safety requirements.

## Celebrating Janmashtami away from home

You can mark Janmashtami in a small way, alone or with friends.

Find your people. Ask a Hindu or Indian student association, an ISKCON or other temple, or a nearby community group whether it holds a midnight celebration or a Dahi Handi game, and whether it streams the midnight program online.

Make a small shrine. A picture or small figure of baby Krishna in a cradle, with a few flowers, is a simple way to join the day. Many residence halls ban open flames, including candles, oil lamps and incense, so check your housing contract and use a battery-powered lamp. If you have questions about religious accommodations, ask your residence life office.

Share something sweet. Offer a little sweet or fruit to the image and share it with friends. If you share food, tell people what is in it.

Watch Dahi Handi, and join only if it is well run. A human pyramid is dangerous without trained organizers, safety gear and cushioning. If you take part, follow the organizers’ instructions.

Call home. A video call with family during the midnight celebration keeps you part of the day.

# Maha Shivaratri

*The great night of Shiva* · Also known as Shivaratri, Mahashivratri, Maha Shivratri

## Quick facts

- **Meaning:** “The great night of Shiva” (maha, great, plus ratri, night), the most important yearly celebration of Shiva for his devotees.
- **Length of observance:** One night of fasting and vigil. In many places the following day brings feasting and fairs.
- **Associated deities:** Shiva, most often worshipped in the form of the lingam. Parvati appears in the marriage story. _(Shaiva and wider Hindu traditions)_
- **Common customs:** Fasting, an all-night vigil, bathing the lingam with milk, honey and water, chanting “Om Namah Shivaya,” and offering bilva leaves.
- **Dietary practices:** Many devotees fast, and how strictly varies. Simple, satvik foods such as fruit and sago dishes are described for those who fast. Practices differ by region, family and tradition. _(Varies)_

## Deities

- **Shiva** _(Shaiva and wider Hindu traditions)_: The god honored on this night. He is worshipped most often as the lingam, a simple stone shaft, and is also shown as a yogi and as the dancing Nataraja.
- **Parvati** _(Varies by community)_: Shiva’s consort. In some accounts the night celebrates their marriage.

## Why celebrate Maha Shivaratri?

Maha Shivaratri, “the great night of Shiva,” is the most important yearly celebration for devotees of Shiva. Many keep a fast, stay awake through the night in prayer, and worship the Shiva lingam, the simple stone form in which Shiva is most often honored, bathing it with milk, honey and water. Devotees believe that worship on this night brings religious and worldly benefits.

Why this night is special is explained in several ways: as the night a blazing pillar of light revealed Shiva’s boundless nature, as the night of Shiva and Parvati’s marriage, or as the night Shiva drank a poison that threatened the world. Each tradition tells it in its own way, and no single story is the only reason.

## A night each month, and a great night once a year

The 14th day of the dark half of every lunar month is sacred to Shiva, and the monthly night is called Shivaratri. Maha Shivaratri, the great night, comes in late winter and is the occasion of particular rejoicing, the year’s most important celebration of Shiva.

Shiva, also called Mahadeva, “the great lord,” is seen in Shaiva tradition as both creator and destroyer. He is worshipped most often as the linga, a simple stone shaft often understood as a symbol of cosmic wholeness, and he is also shown as a yogi and as the dancer Nataraja.

## The pillar of light

_Tradition: Shaiva tradition; the Linga Purana and the Shiva Purana_

One well-known story tells of a time when Brahma and Vishnu argued over which of them was greater. A blazing pillar of light appeared before them, with no beginning and no end. Vishnu took the form of a boar and dived to find its base, and Brahma took the form of a swan and flew up to find its top, but neither could. They bowed to Shiva, who had appeared as the pillar and is honored in it as the linga.

The Linga Purana and the Shiva Purana tell this story, and Shaiva festival tradition associates it with Maha Shivaratri.

## Other stories: the poison and the marriage

_Tradition: Varies by tradition_

Other stories are told about the night in different places. In one, the gods and demons churned the ocean and a deadly poison rose from it; Shiva drank it to protect the world, and it turned his throat blue. In another, the night celebrates the marriage of Shiva and Parvati, a festival of divine union. Many people know more than one.

## The hunter who worshipped without knowing

_Tradition: Shiva Purana_

The Shiva Purana tells of a hunter named Gurudruha who, not knowing it was Shivaratri, went into the forest to find food for his family and spent the night in a bilva tree beside a pond. As he kept watch through the night, leaves and drops of water fell from the tree onto a linga below. He spared the animals that came to the pond when they promised to return, and each of them kept its promise. Shiva appeared, blessed the hunter and freed the animals.

The story is told to show that worship offered on this night is blessed, even when it is offered without knowing.

## The vigil, the fast and the lingam

On Maha Shivaratri many devotees fast, keep a vigil through the night and worship the Shiva lingam. In temples, priests bathe the lingam with offerings such as milk, yogurt, honey and turmeric, rinsing it with water between each, and then decorate it with flowers and garlands. Bilva leaves are a favorite offering, and devotees chant “Om Namah Shivaya,” sing hymns and tell stories of Shiva.

The Shiva Purana describes keeping the vigil through the four watches of the night, with worship in each. The day after the vigil is marked with feasting and fairs in some places.

## Fasting and food

_Tradition: Varies by region, family and tradition_

Fasting is part of Maha Shivaratri for many devotees, and how strictly people fast varies. Maharashtra Tourism describes a strict or partial fast with simple, satvik foods such as fruit and sago dishes. Fasting practices vary by family and tradition.

In many places the day after the vigil is a day of feasting.

## Shiva’s night in different places

_Tradition: Regional; varies by community_

Kashmiri Pandits call the festival Herath, from Hara, a name of Shiva, and ratri, night, and mark it with special rituals and night-long prayers in the home. Among South Indian Lingayats, the day after brings gifts to the guru.

At temples such as Chidambaram, annual dance festivals offer “worship through dance.” In the United States, some temples hold all-night vigils with the full bathing of the lingam, and one community has developed an offering of dance to Shiva as Nataraja.

## Celebrating Maha Shivaratri away from home

You can keep Maha Shivaratri meaningfully in a residence hall with a few simple choices.

Find your people. Ask a Hindu or Indian student association or a nearby Shiva temple whether it holds an evening observance or an all-night vigil.

Worship without a flame. Many residence halls ban open flames, including candles, oil lamps and incense, so check your housing contract. A small image or lingam with a bowl of water, flowers and leaves needs no flame. If you have questions about religious accommodations, ask your residence life office.

Plan any vigil around your classes and your roommates.

Fasting practices vary by family and tradition.

Stay connected. A video call with family, or a shared meal afterward, keeps you part of the celebration.

# Makara Sankranti

*A solar festival of harvest and renewal* · Also known as Sankranti, Makar Sankranti

## Quick facts

- **Meaning:** “Sankranti” means “transition.” Makara Sankranti marks the Sun’s entry into the sign of makara (Capricorn) in the Hindu solar calendar. By tradition it begins uttarayana, the Sun’s northward course; the astronomical turn comes at the December solstice, a few weeks earlier.
- **Length of observance:** One day, with related festivals around it: Lohri the evening before, and Pongal, a four-day festival, in Tamil Nadu. _(Varies by region)_
- **Associated deities:** Surya, the Sun god, in many regions. In Tamil Pongal, Indra is honored on the first day. _(Varies by region)_
- **Common customs:** Sharing sesame-and-jaggery sweets, bathing in sacred rivers, bonfires, rangoli or kolam, honoring cattle, and flying kites, especially in Gujarat.
- **Signature foods:** Sesame-and-jaggery sweets in Maharashtra and Karnataka, rice boiled with milk and jaggery in Tamil Nadu (Pongal), and sweets made with new rice in West Bengal. _(Regional)_

## Deities

- **Surya** _(Hindu traditions across India)_: The Sun god, honored with thanks at harvest time and on Makara Sankranti, the day the Sun enters makara in the Hindu solar calendar.
- **Indra** _(Tamil Pongal)_: Honored as the god of rain on Bhogi, the first day of Pongal.

## Why celebrate Makara Sankranti?

Makara Sankranti is a solar festival of harvest and renewal. In the Hindu solar calendar it marks the Sun’s entry into the sign of makara, or Capricorn, and by tradition it opens uttarayana, the Sun’s northward course. Astronomically, the Sun’s turn comes at the December solstice, a few weeks earlier. Because the festival follows the Sun rather than the Moon, it falls at nearly the same point each year.

Families give thanks for the harvest, share sesame-and-jaggery sweets, bathe in rivers, light bonfires and, in some places, fly kites. The season is celebrated under different names across India, from Pongal in Tamil Nadu to Lohri in Punjab, Uttarayan in Gujarat and Magh Bihu in Assam. Each is its own celebration, kept apart in this guide.

## A solar festival: tradition and astronomy

Sankranti means “transition.” Makara Sankranti is the Sun’s transition into makara in the Hindu solar reckoning, which follows the Sun’s position against the stars. By tradition, uttarayana, the half of the year in which the Sun travels north, is counted from this day. It is regarded as an auspicious time, and the days around it are seen as a favorable moment for new undertakings such as weddings.

This is a traditional reckoning, not the astronomical turn of the Sun. At the December solstice, a few weeks before Makara Sankranti, the Sun reaches its most southerly track in the sky, and the U.S. Naval Observatory explains that it then begins its slow march northward. Britannica notes that the festival began as a winter-solstice holiday, but because it is reckoned by the stars, the two dates have drifted apart over the centuries. Unlike most Hindu festivals, which follow a calendar based on both Moon and Sun, it follows the solar calendar, so it moves very little from year to year.

## One solar moment, many festivals

_Tradition: Regional; varies by community_

The Sun’s entry into makara is marked across India, but under different names and with different customs. They are related celebrations, not one festival with many labels.

In Gujarat it is Uttarayan, known for kite flying and the International Kite Festival in Ahmedabad. In Maharashtra and the Deccan it is Til Sankranti, when sesame and jaggery sweets are shared. In West Bengal it is Poush Sankranti, celebrated with sweets made from new rice. In Punjab, Lohri is celebrated the evening before, around a bonfire. In Tamil Nadu the season brings the four-day harvest festival of Pongal, and in Assam, Magh or Bhogali Bihu brings feasts and a bonfire called the meji. In Kerala, devotees of Ayyappan mark the day with Makaravilakku and a pilgrimage to Sabarimala.

In Telangana and Andhra Pradesh, Kanuma, the festival of cattle, is part of Sankranti. In Telangana it falls on the third day, when cattle and farm tools are worshipped and cattle processions are held, and in Andhra Pradesh bulls are decorated and paraded.

People describe the relationship among these in different ways: some see regional names for one observance, others see separate festivals that share a season. Here each keeps its own name.

## Pongal: four days of thanks

_Tradition: Tamil Nadu and the Tamil diaspora_

Pongal is a four-day harvest festival of Tamil Nadu, celebrated by Tamils around the world. It follows the Tamil solar calendar and opens the month of Thai. The name comes from a Tamil word meaning “to boil over,” a sign of abundance, and the central dish is rice cooked with milk and jaggery until it overflows.

The four days are Bhogi, for Indra, when old things are cleared out and homes cleaned; Surya Pongal, for the Sun; Mattu Pongal, for the cattle that help with the harvest, who are decorated and fed; and Kaanum Pongal, for family and friends. Britannica traces the festival to the Sangam age, when it was called Thai Niradal, and notes that some scholars link it to Vedic first-fruit rites.

## Lohri: the evening before

_Tradition: Punjab and northern India_

Lohri is celebrated the evening before Makar Sankranti, mainly in Punjab, Haryana, Himachal Pradesh and Delhi. Families and neighbors gather around a bonfire lit at sunset, with songs and dancing, to mark the end of the winter season. Hindus and Sikhs both celebrate it.

The name is explained in more than one way: some link it to an iron griddle, others to Dulla Bhatti, a folk hero from the Mughal period. Britannica notes that the exact origin of the festival is unclear.

## Customs of the season

A few customs appear in many places, each with local forms. Many people bathe in rivers, especially where rivers meet; pilgrims go to Gangasagar, where the Ganges reaches the sea, and to Prayagraj for the Magh Mela. Sesame and jaggery sweets are exchanged as a gesture of goodwill. Bonfires are lit in several regions, and homes are cleaned and decorated with rangoli or kolam.

Cattle, honored as helpers of the harvest, are garlanded or decorated and fed. In Gujarat the sky fills with kites. Britannica notes that the many reasons people give for flying kites are suggestions, and that the pastime has simply become part of the festival.

## Sesame, jaggery and new rice

_Tradition: Regional_

Sweet foods mark the season, and each region has its own. In Maharashtra, families exchange tilgul, sesame-and-jaggery sweets, with a wish to speak sweetly to one another. In Karnataka, people share ellu bella, a mix of sesame, fried gram, dried coconut, peanuts and jaggery, and make both sweet and savory pongal.

In Tamil Nadu, rice cooked with milk and jaggery is the festival’s signature dish. A sweet version made with lentils and jaggery is called chakkara pongal, and a savory one made with rice and lentils is ven pongal. In West Bengal, sweets are made from the new rice. What a family makes depends on its region and its own tradition.

## Celebrating Makar Sankranti away from home

You can keep the festival going away from home with a few simple choices.

Find your people. Ask a regional student association or a nearby temple whether it plans a Sankranti, Pongal or Lohri gathering.

Share sesame sweets thoughtfully. Sesame is one of the nine major food allergens in the United States, so tell friends what is in what you share, and if you are buying sweets, check the label.

Cook in a shared kitchen. A pot of pongal is a good dish to try, but cooking in residence-hall rooms is often restricted, so use a shared kitchen if your housing allows.

Leave the bonfire for public events. Many residence halls ban open flames, including candles and lamps, so check your housing contract before you light or hang anything. If you have questions about religious accommodations, ask your residence life office.

Fly kites carefully. Use ordinary string, never glass-coated string, and fly only where local rules allow, away from roads and overhead wires.

Call home. A video call with family at festival time keeps you part of the celebration.

# Rama Navami

*A festival honoring the birth of Rama* · Also known as Ram Navami, Sri Rama Navami

## Quick facts

- **Meaning:** “Ninth of Rama”: the birthday of Rama, hero of the Ramayana, on the ninth day of the bright half of the month of Chaitra and the last day of spring Navaratri.
- **Length of observance:** One day, the ninth and final day of spring Navaratri.
- **Associated deities:** Rama, worshipped with his wife Sita, his brother Lakshmana and his devotee Hanuman. _(Vaishnava and wider Hindu traditions)_
- **Common customs:** Cleaning and bathing, worship at home and in the temple, a cradle for baby Rama, readings of the Ramayana, Ramlila, processions, and in southern India a symbolic wedding of Rama and Sita.
- **Signature foods:** Panaka, a sweet jaggery drink, and spiced buttermilk, served to the public by devotees. _(Some temples)_

## Deities

- **Rama** _(Vaishnava and wider Hindu traditions)_: The hero of the Ramayana and righteous king of Ayodhya, regarded as the seventh avatar of Vishnu, whose birth the day celebrates.
- **Sita** _(South India and the diaspora)_: Rama’s wife, whose wedding to Rama is re-enacted at many temples, especially in southern India.
- **Hanuman** _(Hindu traditions across India)_: Rama’s devoted servant, shown on bended knee at Rama’s altar; in many ways he is worshipped more widely than Rama himself.

## Why celebrate Rama Navami?

Rama Navami celebrates the birth of Rama, the hero of the Ramayana, on the ninth day of the bright half of the month of Chaitra. It is the ninth and last day of the spring Navaratri. Vaishnavas and many other Hindus honor Rama as an avatar of Vishnu and as the righteous king of Ayodhya whose life the Ramayana tells.

Families clean their homes, bathe and worship, and some rock a small image of baby Rama in a cradle. Temples hold readings of the Ramayana, dramas of Rama’s story and processions and, especially in southern India, a symbolic wedding of Rama and Sita. The day is a reminder of the qualities for which Rama is admired: courage, kindness, justice and devotion to duty.

## Rama’s birth in the Ramayana

_Tradition: Valmiki Ramayana_

The Ramayana of Valmiki tells how King Dasharatha of Ayodhya, who had no son, performed a great sacrifice to ask for heirs. At the same time, the gods, threatened by the demon king Ravana, asked Vishnu to be born as a human to defeat him. A divine being rose from the sacrificial fire with a vessel of payasa, a sweet milk-and-rice dish, which Dasharatha shared among his three queens. In time, Rama was born to Kausalya, Bharata to Kaikeyi, and the twins Lakshmana and Shatrughna to Sumitra. The text places Rama’s birth in the month of Chaitra, on the ninth day, under the star Punarvasu.

The epic describes the rejoicing in Ayodhya at the births.

## The last day of spring Navaratri

Rama Navami is the ninth and final day of the spring Navaratri, also called Chaitra or Vasanta Navaratri. It is the spring counterpart of the nine nights of Navaratri that are more widely celebrated in the autumn, and in the spring Navaratri the goddess Durga is worshipped in her nine forms.

## A day of worship at home and in the temple

Many people begin the day by cleaning the home and bathing, then worship at home, bathing a murti of Rama. Some place a murti of the child Rama in a cradle and rock it. Devotees visit temples to see and be seen by the deity, and many fast or eat only fruit or simple offerings and recite mantras to Rama.

The Ramayana, or another telling of Rama’s life such as Tulsidas’s Ramcharitmanas, is read or recited aloud, sometimes without a break, and Ramlilas, dramatic retellings of Rama’s story, are staged. Temples carry murtis in procession around their grounds, and devotees sing and give to charity.

Hindus regard Ayodhya as Rama’s birthplace and childhood home, and the city celebrates Rama Navami with particular pomp. The festival is especially popular in Uttar Pradesh, where Ayodhya lies.

## The wedding of Rama and Sita

_Tradition: South India and American temples_

In southern India, many people regard Rama Navami as the wedding anniversary of Rama and Sita. At temples, priests perform a symbolic wedding, called a kalyanotsavam, and the murtis of Rama and Sita are carried in procession.

In the United States, at one Michigan temple, families of the congregation sponsor the wedding as the families of the bride and the groom.

## Fasting and food

_Tradition: Varies by tradition and family_

Many devotees fast on Rama Navami, and some eat only fruit or simple offerings. Hinduism Today describes devotees making and serving panaka, a sweet drink made with jaggery, lime, ginger and cardamom, and spiced buttermilk to the public, and some temples preparing sweets such as khoa. Fasting practices vary by family and tradition.

## Celebrating Rama Navami away from home

Rama Navami can be marked simply, even in a small room.

Find your people. Ask a Hindu or Indian student association or a nearby temple whether it holds a Rama Navami program, a Ramayana reading or a Sita Rama kalyanam.

Worship without a flame. Many residence halls ban open flames, including candles, oil lamps and incense, so check your housing contract. A battery-powered lamp is a safer way to light a small shrine. If you have questions about religious accommodations, ask your residence life office.

Make a small cradle. A picture or small figure of baby Rama in a simple cradle is a way to join the day’s custom.

Read together. Read a passage of the Ramayana aloud, or sing a bhajan with friends or over a video call with family.

Fasting practices vary by family and tradition.
