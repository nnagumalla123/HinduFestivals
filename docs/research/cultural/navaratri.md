# Source pack: Navaratri (Sharad Navaratri)

Scope: the autumn (Sharad) Navaratri. **Vijayadashami/Dussehra is a separate guide** (PRD section 2); this pack only records where the sources link the two. Chaitra (spring) Navaratri is noted only to avoid conflation.

## 2. Claim map

### Why it is celebrated and what it means

| ID | Claim | Region/tradition | Sources | Support | Flags |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | Navaratri means "nine nights" and honours the divine feminine, centrally the goddess Durga. Britannica says it honours her nine forms (Shailaputri, Brahmacharini, Chandraghanta, Kushmanda, Skandamata, Katyayani, Kalaratri, Mahagauri, Siddhidatri), each associated with a quality such as strength or wisdom. The festival is observed by Hindus across South Asia and the diaspora, including large public celebrations in the US. | Hindu, Shakta-centred; pan-Indian | N1, N4, N5, N8 | Strong | Some traditions name different forms or do not use the "nine forms" scheme; say "in many traditions". Durga Puja is a related but distinct framing (C6). |
| C2 | Navaratri is observed several times a year (Britannica names four: Chaitra, Ashadha, Ashvina and Magha). The autumn one, in Ashvina, is the most widely observed, called Sharad Navaratri; Ashadha and Magha are "Gupt" (secret) Navaratri; Chaitra (spring) Navaratri begins on the lunar day that is also the new year in some regions (see Ugadi pack). Because the calendar is lunar, the festival can last eight days in some years. | Hindu calendars | N1 (also U1 for Chaitra) | Moderate (Britannica only, with U1 for Chaitra) | Other sources anchor the festival differently: a solar "autumn equinox" (N6), the new moon of the Tamil month Purattasi (N7), "the first new moon of early autumn" (N8). Do not anchor it to a solar date; say "in autumn, on the lunar calendar". |
| C3 | The central story: the buffalo demon Mahishasura, protected by a boon that no male could defeat him, drove out the gods, who pooled their powers to create a warrior goddess (Durga), arming her with their weapons. In Britannica's retelling he shape-shifts in battle for nine days and she beheads him on the tenth, Vijayadashami. The main text is the Devi-Mahatmya (part of the Markandeya Purana, about the 5th or 6th century CE, per Britannica). Tamil accounts have the gods' consorts (Lakshmi, Saraswati, Parvati) lend their power and say the fight lasted ten days, with the victory at midnight of the tenth (N7). | Shakta; Devi tradition; narrative | N2, N5, N6, N7, N8, N10 | Strong for the story; **Weak for the nine-night link** | **The Devi-Mahatmya cantos read (N10: 82, 91, 92, 93) tell the Mahisha story and describe worship of the goddess, including "the great annual worship that is performed in autumn time" (canto 92), but never mention nine nights, "Navaratri", Vijayadashami, or Rama.** Cantos 83-90, where the killing is told, were not read. Siddha Yoga says the text "recounts the tradition of Navaratri" (N8); the read cantos do not support that. **Battle length differs:** N5 says nine days; N6 and N7 say ten; N2 nine then the tenth; the text itself (canto 82) opens with a war of a hundred years. State the story as "in the Devi-Mahatmya the goddess defeats Mahishasura; tradition links the victory to the festival", not a specific day count. |
| C4 | Durga is named in very old Sanskrit hymns (Britannica cites the Rigveda and Atharvaveda) and two Puranas are devoted to the goddess (the Devi Mahatmya and the Devi Bhagavata Purana). Unlike many goddesses, Durga is not usually described as a male deity's consort except in a Durga Puja myth. | Scholarly, Hindu | N2 | Moderate (single source) | The dating is Britannica's. Verify before use. |

### Traditions

| ID | Claim | Region/tradition | Sources | Support | Flags |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C5 | The nights are commonly divided into three phases: Durga, then Lakshmi, then Saraswati, each phase of three nights. Some worship all nine nights as nine forms of Durga instead. | Pan-Indian, varies | N1, N5, N6, N7, N8 | Strong | Many families follow one scheme or the other; say so. |
| C6 | **Bengal and eastern India (Durga Puja):** large clay images of Durga with Mahishasura in decorated temporary pavilions (pandals) and temples; the final day is Vijayadashami, with immersion of the images in water, a mirror ritual of farewell, and married women applying vermilion to the goddess and each other. Britannica calls Durga Puja a ten-day festival (Kolkata's observance is on UNESCO's intangible-heritage list, 2021). A strongly held Bengali belief, which Britannica says is not rooted in scripture, is that Durga visits her parental home with her children and returns to Shiva on the tenth day. | Bengal, Assam, Odisha, Tripura and eastern India | N1, N3, N4 (Navaratri also called Durga Puja), N5 | Strong for the broad picture; Moderate for details | **Length conflict:** Britannica says ten days; a temple blog (N9) says five days (Shashthi to Dashami). The "five main days" framing is plausible but not confirmed by a reputable source. Britannica's page mentions the soil-from-a-threshold custom; sensitive, README question 4. |
| C7 | **Gujarat:** garba and dandiya raas circle dances around a lamp, with fairs and communal feasting, are central. In the US, public garba events are a major way diaspora communities observe it. | Gujarat; diaspora | N1, N4, N5 | Strong | Siddha Yoga mentions garba without naming a region (N8). Do not imply garba is universal. |
| C8 | **South India (Golu):** families, especially in Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Karnataka (also parts of Kerala), arrange dolls and figures on odd-numbered stepped platforms, depicting deities and scenes such as from the Ramayana, invite neighbours and friends (including girls and married women), share offerings and sing. Names: Bommai Golu (Tamil), Bommala Koluvu (Telugu), Gombe Habba (Kannada, per a secondary source). Ayudha Puja on the ninth day honours tools, books and instruments; many families do Saraswati Puja on the ninth and learning-related rites on the tenth. | Tamil Nadu, Andhra, Telangana, Karnataka | N1, N6, N7 | Strong for the practice; Moderate for details | **Details conflict:** the pot (kalasam) is on the top step (N6) or the middle or second step (N7); the word "golu" is said to come from "to show off" (N6, a single, family-based source) or is translated "court of dolls" (N7). Origins "believed" to date from the Vijayanagara kingdom (N7) are weakly sourced. Kanya-puja-like honouring of girls appears in a Tamil family account (N6) and a Singapore Tamil account (N7), as well as in N1 without a region, so it is **not only a northern custom**. |
| C9 | **North India:** many people fast; Kanya Puja (Kanjak) on the eighth or ninth day honours young girls as forms of the goddess (washing feet, a meal of puri, semolina halva and black chickpeas, gifts); Ramlila plays and the burning of effigies of Ravana (with Meghanada and Kumbhakarna) mark Dussehra in the north. In Karnataka the ten-day celebration is called Dussehra in some places. | North India; Karnataka | N1, N5, N9 | Strong | Ramlila and effigies belong to the Vijayadashami guide. |

## 3. Flagged claims

- **Unsupported or contradicted in the read primary text:** that the Devi-Mahatmya describes a nine-night festival (N8). Cantos 83-90 must be read before the guide states how the text frames the battle length.
- **Conflicts:** battle length (nine vs ten days); Durga Puja length (ten vs five days); kalasam placement; golu etymology; whether Kanya Puja is northern only; month/season anchoring.
- **Single-source or weak:** Golu from the Vijayanagara era; marapachi dolls "put to sleep" on the tenth day; a yogurt-based offering to Durga on the last day (a single oral-history recollection); "Navaratri four times a year" is Britannica-only; Ayudha Puja's origin story.
- **Over-generalisation risk:** vegetarian or fasting as universal; Rama-Ravana as the Navaratri story; "nine forms" as universal; garba as universal; "Navaratri equals Durga Puja".
- **Not a source for any claim:** health claims about fasting (N9).

## 4. Candidate alias and basics notes (not published)

Britannica's alternate names: "Navaratri" and "Sharad Navratri". The existing alias "Navratri" is a spelling variant; supported. Existing provisional deities (Durga, Lakshmi, Saraswati) match the three-phase scheme (C5) and N1; "Mahishasura" is the demon, not a deity. The deity list must state that nights are assigned differently across traditions.

## 5. Gaps and questions

1. Read Devi-Mahatmya cantos 83-90 (the Mahishasura killing) and check the text's own framing of days and season; consider a second translation (Swami Jagadiswarananda or Madugula) since Wisdomlib's Pargiter text is 1904.
2. A second, reputable food and fasting source (university, temple board or a museum) beyond Britannica and one temple blog.
3. Verify Britannica's claims on Navaratri frequency (C2) and Durga's textual references (C4) against a scholarly source.
4. Durga Puja length: confirm with a Bengali cultural institution or UNESCO's description.
5. Decide whether Golu details come from several family accounts (N6, N7) or are stated only at the level Britannica gives.
