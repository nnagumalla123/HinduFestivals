## Source-critical notes (kept out of the public text)
- **Diwali: Why celebrate Diwali?.** core summary rests on Britannica plus Princeton and Brandeis, which largely echo the general account (not independent confirmation).
- **Diwali: Rama comes home to Ayodhya.** the Valmiki chapters on the return (sargas 127 and 128 in the Murthy prose translation, valmikiramayan.net) describe flags, flowers, scented water, a procession and a coronation, and no festival of lights. The lamp-lit homecoming is a tradition ('said to' in Britannica; stated without hedging by Princeton, Brandeis and HAF). Public text therefore says 'the tradition says' and does not cite Valmiki.
- **Diwali: Krishna and the demon Narakasura.** the Bhagavata Purana chapter (10.59, Bhaktivedanta Book Trust translation) tells the story without mentioning lamps or a festival; the link to the day is traditional (Britannica, Princeton, HAF).
- **Diwali: Lakshmi finds her way in.** Bhagavata Purana 8.8 tells of Lakshmi rising from the churning of the ocean and choosing Vishnu but makes no Diwali link; the link is traditional. Not stated publicly.
- **Diwali: Krishna lifts Govardhana Hill.** the Bhagavata chapters (10.24 and 10.25) do not mention lamps, Annakut or a festival; the link to the day is traditional.
- **Diwali: Diwali, day by day.** day names and order follow Britannica; HAF's list adds a sixth day (Govatsa Dvadashi) at the start. Other regional counts are not sourced.
- **Diwali: Many Diwalis across India.** Kali in Bengal: Britannica plus a University of Birmingham student blog by a Bengali community member (see the Kali deity entry).
- **Diwali: Many Diwalis across India.** HAF says 'some regions' mark Diwali as the new year without naming them; Britannica ties it to the Vikrama calendar; the Malaiya page says the Vikrama year begins at Diwali in Gujarat (the region named in the public text).
- **Diwali: Jain and Sikh meanings.** Jain paragraph verified against a Jain scholar's page (Malaiya, Colorado State University), which cites an early Jain text for the kings' lamps, plus Britannica, Princeton and Brandeis. Sikh paragraph verified against SikhNet (Bandi Chhor Divas) plus Britannica and Brandeis. The Sikh release year differs across sources and is omitted.
- **Diwali: quick fact "Length of observance".** Britannica says five days and calls Lakshmi Puja the main day; HAF's text says five but its list shows six. Public wording softened to 'a central observance for many communities' (owner direction).
- **Diwali: deity Kali.** Kali in Bengal is now supported by Britannica and by a University of Birmingham student blog written by a Bengali community member (a personal account, so tradition-specific but informal). Both say Kali is worshipped on the same new-moon night; neither is a scholarly source.
- **Ugadi: Why celebrate Ugadi?.** core summary rests on Britannica plus two Telugu newspapers and a US temple page (separate publishers).
- **Ugadi: Why celebrate Ugadi?.** 'take the sweet and the bitter together' paraphrases The Hans India's reading of the pachadi.
- **Ugadi: A new year in spring.** the sixty-year cycle of named years is now supported by Telangana Today (Telugu context), The Hans India (year names in use) and the 1911 Britannica entry on Hindu chronology (a sixty-year Jupiter cycle with special names such as Prabhava). The mechanism and the count differ between north and south India, so the public text says only that years run in a cycle of sixty with their own names.
- **Ugadi: A new year in spring.** spring and the planting season are from Britannica. 'A fresh start' is supported by two US temple pages (HTCI, Sri Venkateswara Temple) and Karnataka Tourism. The Hans India's neem-blossom, farmers-awaiting-showers and housewarming details were removed (single source).
- **Ugadi: Brahma and the beginning of time.** Britannica says 'according to some Hindu texts'; a popular magazine cites the Brahma Purana without a passage. OPTIONAL: Kane, History of Dharmasastra (candidate, unverified).
- **Ugadi: How the day unfolds.** the forecast areas (weather, harvests, general fortune) draw on Karnataka Tourism, The Hans India and a temple page; Britannica says only that people hear the almanac recited.
- **Ugadi: Ugadi pachadi: the taste of a year (Telugu).** ingredient lists and taste meanings differ across Britannica, Telangana Today, The Hans India, a US temple page and Deccan Chronicle (which lists six flavors without ingredients); the public text says families vary.
- **Ugadi: Bevu-bella in Karnataka (Kannada).** resolved with a Kannada-region source. Karnataka Tourism (Government of Karnataka) describes bevu-bella as a simple neem-and-jaggery mixture and says ugadi pachadi is more popular in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana but also made in many Karnataka households; The Hans India agrees (jaggery-and-neem version more popular with Kannadigas). Britannica uses 'bevu-bella' as the Karnataka name for ugadi pachadi itself, so the public text acknowledges both usages.
- **Ugadi: Ugadi, Gudi Padwa and Chaitra Navaratri.** the flag's 'victory' meaning is Britannica's; a popular magazine adds a Maratha link, omitted (single source).
- **Navaratri: Why celebrate Navaratri?.** Britannica plus the HPI digest (Hinduism Today family, a short excerpt of a news article) and the Pluralism Project: separate publishers, but HPI adds little independent detail.
- **Navaratri: Durga and Mahishasura.** cantos 82, 91, 92 and 93 (Pargiter translation) were read; they do not mention nine nights, Navaratri or Vijayadashami, though canto 92 mentions a great annual worship of the goddess in autumn. Siddha Yoga's claim that the text 'recounts the tradition of Navaratri' is not supported by the cantos read and is not used.
- **Navaratri: Fasting: optional, and different for everyone.** Britannica supports sattvic eating and daytime fasts; the HPI digest says 'many' in the north fast; a young Tamil-family writer says fasting is 'not how everybody observes' the festival.
- **Navaratri: Durga Puja in Bengal and eastern India.** the ten-day span, Mahalaya as day one, worship beginning on Shashthi and the last five days as the primary period of veneration are stated in Britannica's Durga Puja article (a separate page from the Navratri and Vijayadashami articles, but the same publisher, so not independent confirmation).
- **Navaratri: Garba and dandiya in Gujarat.** 'around a lamp' is from the HPI digest; Britannica says only that the dances are popular in Gujarat. Pluralism notes large public garba celebrations among American Hindus.
- **Navaratri: Golu in South India.** rewritten to rely on Britannica (stepped platforms, odd tiers, Ramayana scenes, neighbors and offerings, Ayudha Puja) and the HPI digest (Golu in some southern states). Marapachi dolls, the kalasam position and 'every family does it differently' came from the Tamil-family accounts (Hinduism Today, BiblioAsia) and are removed.
