> **Revision note 3 (final editorial pass, 2026-11-08):** (1) The December solstice and the Sun's northward turn are now cited to a new source, **MS17**, the U.S. Naval Observatory page "Sunrise and Sunset Times Near the Solstices" (https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/rs_solstices). **Content read status:** read in full in the built-in browser (page text extracted directly), 2026-11-08. It says the winter solstice falls on or about December 21, when the Sun appears to move along its most southern track in the sky; that the Earth's axis is tilted about 23.4°; and that the Sun's southward drift stops at the solstice, "after which the Sun begins its slow march northward". It does not give a gap to the festival; the "few weeks earlier" comparison is the editor's inference from that date and Britannica's mid-January timing, and no date is printed in the guide. **Terms review status:** no terms page read; the page shows no copyright or reuse notice and describes itself as an official U.S. Navy site; paraphrase and link only. Other pages tried: NASA "Tropical Solstice Shadows" loaded through the fetch tool (subagent report, not read directly) and agrees on the southernmost position but does not mention the northward movement, so it is not cited; the NASA Earth Observatory "Winter and Summer Solstice" page says days lengthen after the solstice (fetch tool) but not the northward movement, so it is not cited; several other NASA and USNO URLs returned 404/500 or 403. (2) Surya's entry no longer implies the astronomical turn occurs on Makara Sankranti. (3) The Telugu-region text is limited to the two sources' Kanuma statements (Telangana: third day, worship of cattle and farm tools, cattle processions; Andhra Pradesh: bulls decorated and paraded); "celebrated over several days" was removed as unsourced. Nothing else Telugu-specific is in the guide.

**Summary of the evidence.** Britannica (one publisher) gives a strong core. Independent support comes from the Hindu American Foundation, Hinduism Today, Brandeis, and two state tourism departments. The real difficulty is **framing**: sources disagree on whether Pongal, Lohri, Bihu and Uttarayan are *the same festival under other names* or *separate festivals on the same solar day* (section 3). Telugu-region (Andhra/Telangana) Sankranti has no readable reputable source yet.

### The framing question (D1)
- **Britannica (MS2)** treats Uttarayan, Til Sankranti, Poush Sankranti, Lohri, Pongal and Makaravilakku as regional observances around one date; **MS4** says Pongal is "Makar Sankranti" in other states.
- **HAF (MS7)** presents one festival "commemorated under different names" yet describes Lohri, Makar Sankranti and Pongal as having their own regional practices; the Pongal page (MS8) says Pongal "corresponds with Makar Sankranti and Lohri".
- **Hinduism Today (MS9)** frames it as one four-day thanksgiving using Tamil Pongal as the template, with Telugu and Punjabi names for it.
- **HPI (MS10)** treats them as regional names for one transit observance.
- **Recommendation:** say that the Sun's entry into Makara is marked across India on or around the same day, **under different names and with distinct customs**, and give Pongal and Lohri their own short sections. Never say "Pongal is Makar Sankranti". This follows the owner's earlier rule against collapsing distinct traditions.

## 4. Distinctions requiring care

- **Solar, not lunar:** hence the near-fixed date. No date in the guide; Atlanta date work stays in the ledger.
- **Lohri is the evening before; Pongal runs four days and has its own sequence; Bihu, Uttarayan and Til Sankranti are separate regional festivals.** Do not list them as aliases.
- **Hindus and Sikhs** both observe Lohri.
- **Religious narrative versus history:** the Bhishma story and the "auspicious uttarayana" belief are Mahabharata-based tradition; Pongal's Sangam-age origin is a scholarly attribution (Britannica).
- **Regional foods:** attribute each to its region; do not generalise Pongal or sesame sweets to "all of India".
- **Animal-related customs:** cattle through fire, buffalo and egg fights: omit (D4).

## 6. Recommended guide sections (quality guideline, not a quota)

1. **Why celebrate? (overview, 80 to 150 words):** the Sun's turn north, harvest thanks, one solar day, many names.
2. **A solar festival (significance):** sankranti as transition, uttarayana, why the date barely moves (no date), Britannica's note on the solstice.
3. **One day, many festivals (regional variation):** Pongal, Lohri, Uttarayan, Til Sankranti, Poush Sankranti, Karnataka's Suggi/ellu bella, Magh Bihu; Telugu regions only if sourced (D5).
4. **Pongal: four days (regional variation):** its own section, since it is a distinct festival.
5. **Customs (traditions):** bathing at sacred rivers, sharing sesame-jaggery, bonfires, honouring cattle, rangoli, kites in Gujarat, each with its region.
6. **Uttarayana and Bhishma (story):** optional, attributed, and gentle (D3).
7. **Food (food and fasting):** sesame-jaggery, Pongal and new-rice dishes by region; **no fasting claim; omit the section if only thin**.
8. **Celebrating away from home (college guide):** community Pongal or Sankranti gatherings, kite events in open spaces, sharing sesame sweets with an allergy note, shared kitchens for Pongal (Pongal in a rice cooker is editorial, not sourced); no open flames.
- **Quick facts:** Meaning; Length of observance ("one day; Pongal lasts four days"); Associated deities (Surya); Common customs; Signature foods.
- **Deity entries:** Surya (Pan-Indian); optionally Indra (Bhogi, Pongal only).
