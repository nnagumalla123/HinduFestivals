## Source-critical notes (kept out of the public text)
- **Makara Sankranti: deity Surya.** HAF also says Saraswati is honored at Makar Sankranti; no other source says so, so it is left out.
- **Makara Sankranti: Why celebrate Makara Sankranti?.** core summary rests on Britannica plus HAF and Brandeis; the Brandeis claim that this is the only Hindu festival on the solar calendar is over-generalised and is not used.
- **Makara Sankranti: A solar festival: tradition and astronomy.** Britannica says the festival began as a winter-solstice holiday and that, because its date now follows sidereal time, the date and the solstice are no longer the same (precession of the equinoxes). The astronomy (the Sun’s most southerly track at the December solstice, then its northward movement) is cited to the U.S. Naval Observatory page “Sunrise and Sunset Times Near the Solstices”, read in full in the browser on 2026-11-08. That page does not give the “few weeks earlier” gap to Makara Sankranti; that comparison rests on the solstice date it gives (on or about December 21) and the festival’s mid-January timing in Britannica, and no date appears in the guide. Owner direction 2026-11-08: the guide separates the traditional uttarayana reckoning from the astronomical solstice in the overview, the quick fact and this section.
- **Makara Sankranti: A solar festival: tradition and astronomy.** Britannica says the festival is on 14 January, or 15 January in leap years; other pages give a range. The guide states no date, and the Atlanta date work stays in the date ledger.
- **Makara Sankranti: Customs of the season.** kite-flying health claims (vitamin D) from Karnataka Tourism are uncited and are left out.
- **Makara Sankranti: Sesame, jaggery and new rice.** Karnataka Tourism’s statement that sesame and jaggery are “natural heat boosters” is unsourced and is not used.
- **Makara Sankranti: One solar moment, many festivals.** the Telangana and Andhra Pradesh sentence rests on one targeted search on 2026-11-08 (owner direction): a News On AIR (Prasar Bharati) item says that in Telangana Kanuma is the festival of cattle on the third day of Sankranti, with cattle and agricultural tools worshipped and cattle processions, and the Chittoor District (Government of Andhra Pradesh) page says Kanuma during Sankranti is celebrated with bulls decorated and paraded. The district page adds that in some parts bulls are chased; that is left out (animal custom).
- **Makara Sankranti: Pongal: four days of thanks.** Britannica’s Pongal article was read only through the start of day one; days two to four rest on its table plus HAF and Hinduism Today.
- **Makara Sankranti: Lohri: the evening before.** Britannica’s Lohri article was read only in its first part (origin and harvest context); the bonfire and community details are from that part and HAF.
- **Maha Shivaratri: Why celebrate Maha Shivaratri?.** core summary rests on Britannica plus the Pluralism Project (Harvard) and two government tourism pages; Britannica’s own legend list is only in an AI-generated summary page, which is not cited.
- **Maha Shivaratri: A night each month, and a great night once a year.** Britannica says the occasion is especially joyful in Magha and, above all, Phalguna; month names differ between lunar calendars, so the guide says only “late winter” and gives no date.
- **Maha Shivaratri: The pillar of light.** the Linga Purana chapter (1.17) and the Shiva Purana chapter (Vidyesvara 7) were read in J. L. Shastri’s translation on wisdomlib; neither names a night, a tithi, a month or Shivaratri. Pluralism says “it was on this night, they say” that the linga of light was revealed. Wikipedia’s claim that the Linga Purana ties it to Maha Shivaratri is not supported by the chapter read.
- **Maha Shivaratri: The pillar of light.** in the Puranas it is Brahma and Vishnu who quarrel; Pluralism’s version says “the gods”. The guide follows the text.
- **Maha Shivaratri: The hunter who worshipped without knowing.** Kotirudra Samhita chapter 40 (J. L. Shastri’s translation, via wisdomlib) names the hunter Gurudruha; some tellings use other names. The translator’s footnote says the story also occurs, with small differences, in the Padma Purana (not checked).
- **Maha Shivaratri: The vigil, the fast and the lingam.** the Shiva Purana chapter (Kotirudra 38) prescribes the four-watch vigil; its month naming follows its own calendar and is not repeated. The Linga Purana’s vrata chapters (1.83, 1.84) prescribe monthly fasts and name no Shivaratri vigil.
- **Holi: Why celebrate Holi?.** core summary rests on Britannica plus HAF, Brandeis and Incredible India (separate publishers).
- **Holi: The burning of Holika.** Britannica says the story is “from the Puranas” without naming a text. The Bhagavata Purana’s Prahlada chapters 7.4 to 7.6 (read) never name Holika, and an abridged Vishnu Purana text (read) has a fire attempt that fails and no Holika; chapters 7.2, 7.3, 7.7 to 7.10 and the Narada and Bhavishya Puranas were not read. Per owner decision 2026-11-08 the guide presents Holika’s burning as a traditional narrative and names no text.
- **Holi: Radha and Krishna.** Britannica ties the play to the Bhagavata Purana and the Gitagovinda and the color association to the Garga Samhita; none of these was read. The popular story is attributed as one (“one popular story”).
- **Holi: A bonfire and a day of color.** Britannica says Holi gives license to deviate from the usual norms and rankings of caste, gender, status and age, and that ordinary order is restored afterward. Owner direction 2026-11-08: the guide softens this so it does not suggest Holi universally suspends social hierarchies; it says only that in some places the play loosens everyday formalities of age and status.
- **Holi: Sweets and drinks.** Britannica lists thandai (almonds, saffron, poppy seeds, cardamom, rose petals) and bhang (“a mild cannabis-based drink”) as separate drinks; Incredible India describes thandai as a cooling drink flavoured with nuts and spices and does not mention bhang. The guide states only that bhang is listed separately and served in some places.
- **Holi: Colors, then and now.** plant colors and the synthetic-pigment warning are Britannica only; the symbolism is Incredible India’s (“said to”). No Holi-specific health source was found.
- **Holi: Holi across India.** all of this is Britannica (one source); the end of its Dol Jatra passage was not read.
- **Rama Navami: quick fact "Signature foods".** one magazine page, with no region given; kosambari and neer mor appear only on a weak blog and are not used.
- **Rama Navami: deity Rama.** the avatar framing belongs to Vaishnava thought; no reputable source read describes differences among Vaishnava traditions, so none are claimed.
- **Rama Navami: Why celebrate Rama Navami?.** core summary rests on Britannica plus Brandeis, the Pluralism Project and Hinduism Today (separate publishers).
- **Rama Navami: The last day of spring Navaratri.** single source for the spring Navaratri link, Britannica (which says Durga is worshipped in nine forms and that autumn is the more popular Navaratri); Hinduism Today’s “sometimes observed for nine days before or after navami” is unexplained and not used. The Navaratri guide covers the autumn festival and the Ugadi guide mentions the spring one; wording is kept consistent.
- **Rama Navami: Rama’s birth in the Ramayana.** Valmiki’s Bala Kanda sargas 16 and 18 were read directly; the site credits the Bala Kanda translation to Desiraju Hanumanta Rao (Murthy translated the Ayodhya, Sundara and Yuddha Kandas). Britannica and the Valmiki prose differ on the fractions of the payasa, so none are stated; the translator’s notes say the order of the four births is debated.
- **Rama Navami: A day of worship at home and in the temple.** Hinduism Today adds that it is common to stay awake all night; that generalisation is unattributed and is left out.
- **Rama Navami: The wedding of Rama and Sita.** the Pluralism essay is undated and describes one temple, so the American example is generic and names no temple. Other traditions mark the wedding on Vivaha Panchami; no reputable source read says so, so it is not mentioned.
